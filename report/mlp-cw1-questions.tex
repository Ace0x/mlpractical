%% Question 6:
\newcommand{\questionSix} {
According to \citealt{Zhang2018}, combining L1 and L2 regularisation can leverage the strengths of both methods to improve model performance. This technique is often referred to as Elastic Net regularisation. The combined loss function can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_1 \sum_{i} |w_i| + \lambda_2 \sum_{i} w_i^2
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{L}$ is the total loss function.
    \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}$ is the original loss function (e.g., cross-entropy loss).
    \item $w_i$ represents the weights of the model.
    \item $\lambda_1$ is the hyperparameter controlling the strength of L1 regularisation, promoting sparsity in the model weights.
    \item $\lambda_2$ is the hyperparameter controlling the strength of L2 regularisation, encouraging smaller weight values and reducing overfitting.
\end{itemize}
The potential benefits of this approach include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sparsity: L1 regularisation can lead to sparse models by driving some weights to zero, effectively performing feature selection.
    \item Stability: L2 regularisation helps to stabilize the model by penalizing large weights, which can improve generalization.
    \item Flexibility: The combination allows for a balance between sparsity and stability, which can be tuned based on the specific dataset and task.
\end{itemize}
In our implementation, we can use a mixing parameter $\alpha$ to control the relative contributions of L1 and L2 regularisation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda \left( \alpha \sum_{i} |w_i| + (1 - \alpha) \sum_{i} w_i^2 \right)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the overall regularisation strength, and $\alpha$ (ranging from 0 to 1) determines the balance between L1 and L2 penalties.
By controlling $\alpha$, we can adjust the model to prioritize either sparsity or stability, depending on the requirements of the task at hand}

The results from L1 and L2 regularization methods provided in Table~\ref{tab:hp_search} show that the two penalties behave very differently with respect to $\lambda$. 
For L1, the best-performing value is $\lambda = 5\times10^{-4}$, which yields the highest validation accuracy (79.5\%)
 and the lowest validation error (0.658) among the stable runs. Increasing $\lambda$ further leads to rapid degradation: 
 at $\lambda \geq 10^{-3}$ the model already performs significantly worse, and at $\lambda \geq 5\times10^{-3}$

For L2, the optimal region is broader. Validation accuracy is highest at $\lambda = 5\times10^{-4}$ (85.1\%), 
while validation error is lowest at $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ (0.453). Suggesting that L2 performs best with moderate regularisation ranging 
from $5\times10^{-4}$--$10^{-3}$,  and is far more tolerant than L1. However, at $\lambda = 5\times10^{-2}$ even L2 collapses, 
showing that excessively large penalties remain undesirable.

Although L2 performs best at moderate $\lambda$, the strong sensitivity of L1 means that any $\lambda$ chosen in this
middle region would cause the L1 term to dominate the combined Elastic Net penalty.
As established by \citealt{Zhang2018}, while L1 provides necessary sparsity, it lacks the grouping effect required for stability 
in the presence of correlated features; the Elastic Net formulation is specifically designed to overcome these limitations by adding the quadratic L2 term. 
Therefore, to properly study the interaction, we require a $\lambda$ small enough that the L1 component does not overwhelm optimisation, 
allowing the stabilizing effect of the L2 component to be observed cleanly.

This motivates the following research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{When $\lambda$ is small enough that neither regulariser dominates, does shifting the Elastic Net mixture toward L1 or toward L2 lead to better generalisation on EMNIST?}
\end{quote}

To answer this, we fix a conservative value of $\lambda = 5\times10^{-5}$, which ensures 
stable training while preserving meaningful contributions from both penalties 
across $\alpha$. Holding $\lambda$ fixed also isolates $\alpha$ as the sole factor 
controlling the balance: L1 promotes sparsity while L2 provides smooth weight shrinkage. 
Varying $\alpha$ therefore directly probes the trade-off between feature selection and coefficient shrinkage described by \citealt{Zhang2018}.

Given the budget of four experiments, we select $\alpha$ values spanning the L2-dominant to L1-dominant regimes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Run 1: $\lambda = 5\times10^{-5}$, $\alpha = 0.90$ (strong L1-dominant)
    \item Run 2: $\lambda = 5\times10^{-5}$, $\alpha = 0.75$ (moderately L1-dominant)
    \item Run 3: $\lambda = 5\times10^{-5}$, $\alpha = 0.50$ (balanced mixture)
    \item Run 4: $\lambda = 5\times10^{-5}$, $\alpha = 0.25$ (L2-dominant)
\end{itemize}

These configurations form a structured sweep across the full mixture range and allow us 
to assess how the relative weighting of sparsity (L1) and shrinkage (L2) influences 
generalisation performance when the overall regularisation strength is held constant}

The analysis presented in this report confirmed that increasing model capacity, whether through width or depth, 
increases the number of learnable parameters, enabling the model to represent more complex functions. 
Wider networks increase parallel representational power, while deeper networks add complexity and non-linearity. 
However, in both cases, capacity grows faster than the amount of available data, which amplifies variance and leads 
to earlier overfitting. This explains why validation error rises with both width and depth despite improved training accuracy, 
aligning with the classical bias--variance trade-off described by Goodfellow et al.~\cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016}

Our experiments demonstrated that several regularisation techniques can mitigate this effect. Dropout with a high inclusion probability ($p = 0.97$) 
achieved the best generalisation performance, improving validation accuracy ($85.4\%$) and reducing the generalisation gap ($0.213$) relative to the baseline.
This diverges from Srivastava et al.~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}, who find that Dropout is particularly effective for image classification tasks, 
but recommend an inclusion probability range of 0.5--0.8, noting that validation error increases as p approaches 1. However, 
their finding that Dropout often outperforms pure weight decay aligns with our observation that Dropout achieved stronger generalisation than either L1 or L2 alone.
Our finding that Elastic Net great validation performance agrees with the original motivation by Zou and Hastie~\cite{Zhang2018}, 
who show that Elastic Net is particularly effective when inputs exhibit local correlations—precisely the case in EMNIST, where neighbouring pixels carry 
redundant information.

Label smoothing also yielded a modest improvement, although it was less effective than Dropout, L2, and Elastic Net based methods. In contrast, 
L1 regularisation alone performed poorly; however, its strong contribution within the Elastic Net configurations suggests that sparsity 
offers benefits for EMNIST, whose images contain substantial redundancy and locally correlated features.

The failure of the custom activation function further highlighted that poor learning dynamics are not always attributable to overfitting. 
In this case, vanishing gradients prevented the model from learning, resulting in uniformly low accuracy and stagnant optimisation.

Future work should involve a more comprehensive grid search over the Elastic Net hyperparameters $(\lambda, \alpha)$ and explore combined regularisation 
approaches---for example, pairing L2 penalties with light Dropout---to determine whether complementary mechanisms can further improve generalisation performance. 
Additionally, beyond the methods tested in this report, several additional regularisation strategies could further improve generalisation. 
One promising direction is \textit{Mixup} \cite{zhang2018mixupempiricalriskminimization}, which constructs synthetic training examples by taking convex combinations 
of pairs of inputs and their corresponding labels. This encourages the model to behave linearly between samples, reduces sensitivity to high-frequency patterns, 
and has been shown to significantly mitigate overfitting in image classification tasks. Incorporating Mixup into the present architecture—or combining it with 
Dropout or weight decay—could provide a complementary form of regularisation and is a natural avenue for future work.}
